Structure Factor Conventions

What Sunny calculates

The magnetic structure factor is the Fourier transform of the dynamical correlations between magnetic moments,

\[\begin{equation} \mathcal{S}^{αβ}(𝐪, ω) ≡ \frac{1}{2π} \int_{-∞}^{∞} e^{-iωt} ⟨\hat{M}_𝐪^{α†}(0) \hat{M}_𝐪^β(t)⟩ dt. \end{equation}\]

The Fourier-transformed magnetic moment density is

\[\begin{equation} \hat{𝐌}_𝐪 ≡ - μ_B \sum_j f_j(𝐪) g_j \hat{𝐒}_{𝐪,j}, \quad \hat{𝐒}_{𝐪,j} ≡ \sum_𝐦 e^{i 𝐪⋅𝐫_{𝐦,j}} \hat{𝐒}_{𝐦,j}. \end{equation}\]

Here $\hat{𝐒}_{𝐦,j}$ is the spin dipole operator at position $𝐫_{𝐦,j}$ for cell $𝐦$ and sublattice $j$ of the chemical cell, the $g_j$-tensor maps effective spin to magnetic moment, $μ_B$ is the Bohr magneton, and $f_j(𝐪)$ is the magnetic form factor.

Sunny reports the normalized structure factor in units of inverse energy,

\[\begin{equation} 𝒮^{αβ}(𝐪, ω) ≡ \frac{1}{N_\mathrm{cells} μ_B^2} \mathcal{S}^{αβ}(𝐪, ω). \end{equation}\]

It is normalized by $N_\mathrm{cells}$, the number of chemical cells in the macroscopic sample. Observe that the scale of $𝒮^{αβ}$ depends on the chemical cell convention, which is set by Crystal. To avoid confusion, Sunny will warn if the chemical cell deviates from ITA crystallographic standards. For example, to model fcc or bcc, one should begin with a cubic cell. Any subsequent calls to reshape_supercell (e.g., to build a primitive cell) will preserve the original chemical cell convention.

Each intensities calculator accepts a measure argument that determines how $𝒮^{αβ}(𝐪, ω)$ is contracted:

  • ssf_trace contracts with $δ^{αβ}$, yielding the trace of $𝒮$.
  • ssf_perp contracts with the projector $δ^{αβ} - q^α q^β/q^2$, as relevant to unpolarized neutron scattering.
  • ssf_custom accepts a user-provided $𝐪$-dependent contraction function. It may return off-diagonal elements or the full 3×3 matrix itself.
  • ssf_custom_bm is similarly flexible, but expects $𝐪$ in the Blume-Maleev axis system.

The default form factors are $f_j(𝐪) = 1$, which model purely localized dipoles. Pass formfactors to select the FormFactor appropriate to each magnetic ion. Pass apply_g=false to replace the magnetic moments $\hat{𝐌}$ by the spin operators $\hat{𝐒}$ as the fundamental observables.

A temperature kT passed to intensities sets the thermal occupation of excitations. An energy kernel models the instrument resolution function $K(\omega, \omega')$. Sunny will use it to calculate broadened intensities,

\[\begin{equation} I(𝐪, ω) = \int K(ω, ω') 𝒮(𝐪, ω') dω'. \end{equation}\]

Unpolarized neutron scattering

Use ssf_perp to calculate the differential cross section for an unpolarized neutron beam. Within the dipole approximation,

\[\begin{equation} \frac{d^2 σ(𝐪, ω)}{dω dΩ} = \frac{q_f}{q_i} \left(\frac{γ r_0}{2}\right)^2 N_\mathrm{cells} \sum_{α,β} \left(δ^{αβ} - \frac{q^α q^β}{q^2}\right) 𝒮^{αβ}(𝐪, ω). \end{equation}\]

Here $𝐪$ and $ω$ denote momentum and energy transferred to the sample. The prefactor $q_f/q_i$ for $𝐪 = 𝐪_i - 𝐪_f$ is provided experimentally. The characteristic magnetic scattering length is $γ r_0 / 2 ≈ 2.69×10^{-5} \mathrm{Å}$, where $γ = 1.913…$ is the neutron gyromagnetic ratio and $r_0$ is the classical electron radius.

Quantum sum rule

Use ssf_trace with apply_g=false to validate the quantum sum rule. For pure spin correlations, the trace of the exact $𝒮^{αβ}(𝐪, ω)$, integrated over all energies and averaged over momenta, satisfies

\[\begin{equation} \left\langle \int_{-∞}^∞ \sum_α 𝒮^{αα}(𝐪, ω) dω \right\rangle_𝐪 = \sum_j s_j (s_j + 1). \end{equation}\]

The sum runs over sublattices $j$ of the chemical cell, each carrying quantum spin $s_j$. The momentum average runs over many Brillouin zones, as detailed in the derivation. Sunny's calculators are only approximate, and may not recover this exact total.

Dissipation and the retarded susceptibility

A general dynamical correlation is,

\[\begin{equation} C(𝐪, ω) ≡ \frac{1}{2π} \int_{-∞}^{∞} e^{-iωt} ⟨\hat{B}_𝐪^†(0) \hat{A}_𝐪(t)⟩ dt. \end{equation}\]

The choice $\hat{A} = \hat{M}^β$ and $\hat{B} = \hat{M}^α$ gives $\mathcal{S}^{αβ}(𝐪, ω)$. Operators evolve in the Heisenberg picture, $\hat{A}(t) ≡ e^{i t \hat{H}} \hat{A} e^{-i t \hat{H}}$, and $⟨\hat{O}⟩ ≡ \mathrm{tr}\, e^{-β \hat{H}} \hat{O} / \mathcal{Z}$ is the thermal average. Let $ϵ_μ$ and $|μ⟩$ denote the exact eigenvalues and eigenstates of $\hat{H}$. In the Lehmann representation,

\[\begin{equation} C(𝐪,ω) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}} e^{-β ϵ_ν} δ(ϵ_μ - ϵ_ν - ω) ⟨ν|\hat{B}^†_𝐪|μ⟩⟨μ|\hat{A}_𝐪 |ν⟩, \end{equation}\]

with implicit summation over $μ$ and $ν$. Each term is a transition $|ν⟩ → |μ⟩$ that absorbs energy $ω$, weighted by the thermal population of the initial state. This weight carries a universal dependence on temperature. On the support of the delta function, $e^{-β ϵ_μ} = e^{-β ω} e^{-β ϵ_ν}$, so the weight factors as $e^{-β ϵ_ν} = [1 + n_B(ω)] (e^{-β ϵ_ν} - e^{-β ϵ_μ})$, where $n_B(ω) ≡ 1 / (e^{β ω} - 1)$ is the Bose function. The result is the fluctuation-dissipation theorem,

\[\begin{equation} C(𝐪, ω) = \frac{1}{π} \left[1 + n_B(ω)\right] χ''(𝐪, ω), \end{equation}\]

where

\[\begin{equation} χ''(𝐪, ω) ≡ \frac{π}{\mathcal{Z}} \left(e^{-β ϵ_ν} - e^{-β ϵ_μ}\right) δ(ϵ_μ - ϵ_ν - ω) ⟨ν|\hat{B}^†_𝐪|μ⟩⟨μ|\hat{A}_𝐪 |ν⟩. \end{equation}\]

The population difference counts absorption minus stimulated emission, i.e., the energy dissipated in the sample. It makes $χ''$ odd: for Hermitian $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$, $χ''(-𝐪, -ω) = -χ''(𝐪, ω)^*$. All asymmetry between positive and negative $ω$ resides in the thermal factor, which satisfies $1 + n_B(-ω) = -e^{-βω} [1 + n_B(ω)]$. This yields detailed balance, $C(-𝐪, -ω) = e^{-βω} C(𝐪, ω)^*$. At zero temperature, the thermal factor becomes the step function $θ(ω)$.

The dissipation is the anti-Hermitian part, $χ'' = (χ - χ^†)/2i$, of the retarded susceptibility,

\[\begin{equation} χ(𝐪, z) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}} \frac{e^{-β ϵ_ν} - e^{-β ϵ_μ}}{ϵ_μ - ϵ_ν - z} ⟨ν|\hat{B}^†_𝐪|μ⟩⟨μ|\hat{A}_𝐪 |ν⟩, \end{equation}\]

evaluated at $z → ω + i0^+$. In the time domain, the population difference arises from a commutator,

\[\begin{equation} χ(𝐪, z) = i \int_0^∞ e^{izt} ⟨[\hat{B}_𝐪^†(t), \hat{A}_𝐪(0)]⟩ dt. \end{equation}\]

By the Kubo formula, $χ$ is the linear response of $⟨\hat{B}_𝐪^†⟩$ to a field that couples to $\hat{A}_𝐪$. Causality makes it analytic in the upper half-plane, which implies the Kramers-Kronig relation,

\[\begin{equation} χ(𝐪, z) = \frac{1}{π} \int_{-∞}^∞ \frac{χ''(𝐪, ω')}{ω' - z} dω'. \end{equation}\]

Calculator details

Each of Sunny's dynamical calculators estimates $χ''(𝐪, ω)$, multiplies by the thermal factor $[1 + n_B(ω)]/π$, and convolves with the kernel. They differ in how $χ''$ is obtained.

Spin wave theory

In linear spin wave theory, quantum spin operators are expressed with Holstein-Primakoff bosons, and the Hamiltonian is expanded to quadratic order, i.e., to leading order in inverse powers of the quantum spin-$s$. For systems constructed with mode = :SUN, Sunny automatically switches to a multi-flavor boson variant of spin wave theory, which captures more single-ion physics.

The population difference $e^{-β ϵ_ν} - e^{-β ϵ_μ}$ in $χ''$ arises from a commutator, and the commutator of two bosons is a c-number. In linear spin wave theory, $χ$ is therefore independent of temperature, and the temperature dependence of the intensity enters only through $1 + n_B(ω)$. The susceptibility $χ(𝐪, z)$ is a finite sum of magnon poles. The calculators differ in how they evaluate it.

  • SpinWaveTheory diagonalizes the quadratic Hamiltonian at each $𝐪$, which gives every pole and its residue. intensities_bands returns these directly, and intensities broadens them.
  • SpinWaveTheorySpiral studies generalized spiral phases, which allow for an incommensurate propagation wavevector $𝐤$. The magnons at $𝐪$ combine three branches, at $𝐪$ and $𝐪 ± 𝐤$ of the co-rotating frame.
  • SpinWaveTheoryLarge uses iterated matrix-vector multiplication to achieve linear-scaling cost in the magnetic cell size. The Lanczos method produces $χ(𝐪, z)$ as a continued fraction, which is evaluated at complex energies without resolving individual poles. Iterations stop when the estimated error is below tol relative to the largest intensity at each $𝐪$. It can be useful for studying systems with large magnetic cells, including systems with long-wavelength structures, or systems with quenched chemical disorder.

Spin wave calculators also accept a regulator $η > 0$, which evaluates $χ$ at $z = ω + iη$ rather than $ω + i0^+$. By the Kramers-Kronig relation, the anti-Hermitian part of $χ(z)$ is the convolution of $χ''$ with a Lorentzian of half width $η$. Because $χ''$ is odd, the broadened poles at $±ϵ$ partially cancel near $ω = 0$, which damps the intensity of soft modes with $ϵ ≲ η$.

Classical spin dynamics

SampledCorrelations estimates $𝒮(𝐪, ω)$ at finite temperature from classical spin dynamics (Landau-Lifshitz, or its SU($N$) generalization). Each call to add_sample! collects statistics from a dynamical trajectory initialized to the current spin configuration. That configuration should be drawn from thermal equilibrium, e.g., via Langevin sampling. Statistical error decreases with the number of decorrelated samples.

sc = SampledCorrelations(sys; measure, dt, energies=range(0, 10, 100))
for _ in 1:nsamples
    for _ in 1:ndecorr
        step!(sys, langevin)
    end
    add_sample!(sc, sys)
end
res = intensities(sc, qpts; energies=:available, kT)

The energies must be a uniform range starting at zero. Its spacing sets the trajectory length, and its maximum sets the sampling interval. A good choice of dt can be estimated from suggest_timestep. Trajectories are finite, so some blurring between neighboring energy bins is unavoidable. Details of the estimator are specified here.

The simulation volume is periodic, so intensities are available only on a discrete grid of wavevectors. For a system of $L_1 × L_2 × L_3$ chemical cells, these are $q_i = n_i / L_i$ in reciprocal lattice units. The intensities function rounds each requested $𝐪$ to the nearest grid point.

Classical trajectories sample the classical correlation, for which the fluctuation-dissipation theorem reads $C_\mathrm{cl}(𝐪, ω) = (kT/πω) χ''(𝐪, ω)$. The quantum thermal factor $1 + n_B(ω)$ is replaced by its high-temperature limit $kT/ω$. Given kT, intensities multiplies by $βω [1 + n_B(ω)]$, which restores the quantum factor and makes the result comparable with spin wave theory. With kT = nothing, the classical correlations are reported unchanged.

Static correlations

The static structure factor $𝒮(𝐪) = \int 𝒮(𝐪, ω) dω$ is the equal-time correlation. Sunny's intensities_static obtains it in one of two ways.

Frequency-dependent calculators evaluate the $ω$ integral, with optional restriction to energy bounds. For SpinWaveTheory and SpinWaveTheorySpiral, the integral becomes a sum over bands, and omits the elastic Bragg weight. For SampledCorrelations, it is a sum over the energy grid, which retains the elastic line at $ω = 0$. With finite kT, the sum is taken after the classical-to-quantum factor. With kT = nothing, it gives the classical equal-time correlations.

The SampledCorrelationsStatic calculator skips the dynamics and instead samples $𝒮(𝐪)$ directly from the classical Boltzmann distribution. This accelerates the calculation, but reflects classical rather than quantum thermal statistics. It is most accurate at higher temperatures, where the fluctuations become effectively classical. The SCGA calculator runs even faster, and makes the additional approximation that spin fluctuations are effectively Gaussian. SCGA can often be quite good when deep in the paramagnetic phase. For both calculators, it is recommended to first call set_spin_rescaling_for_static_sum_rule!.

Derivation of the quantum sum rule

Integrating over all energies yields the equal-time correlation,

\[\begin{equation} \int_{-∞}^∞ \mathcal{S}^{αα}(𝐪, ω) dω = ⟨\hat{M}_𝐪^{α†} \hat{M}_𝐪^α⟩, \end{equation}\]

with summation on $α$ implied. For pure spin correlations, $\hat{𝐌}_𝐪$ is replaced by $\hat{𝐒}_𝐪 = \sum_{𝐦,j} e^{i 𝐪⋅𝐫_{𝐦,j}} \hat{𝐒}_{𝐦,j}$, and the right-hand side becomes a double sum over sites,

\[\begin{equation} ⟨\hat{𝐒}_𝐪^† ⋅ \hat{𝐒}_𝐪⟩ = \sum_{𝐦,j} \sum_{𝐦',j'} e^{i 𝐪⋅(𝐫_{𝐦',j'} - 𝐫_{𝐦,j})} ⟨\hat{𝐒}_{𝐦,j} ⋅ \hat{𝐒}_{𝐦',j'}⟩. \end{equation}\]

Averaging over momenta $𝐪$ eliminates every term with $𝐫_{𝐦',j'} ≠ 𝐫_{𝐦,j}$. Within a single Brillouin zone, the phases cancel between distinct cells $𝐦 ≠ 𝐦'$. Between distinct sublattices $j ≠ j'$ of the same cell, the average must extend over many Brillouin zones. Only the on-site terms survive, and each contributes $⟨\hat{𝐒}^2⟩ = s_j(s_j+1)$. There are $N_\mathrm{cells}$ such terms for each sublattice $j$, and the normalization of $𝒮$ divides by $N_\mathrm{cells}$. This recovers the sum rule stated above.

The calculators recover this total only in part.

  • Spin wave theory omits the elastic Bragg weight from its band intensities. For a polarized ferromagnet, the inelastic weight per spin is exactly $s$, and adding the elastic weight $s^2$ recovers $s(s+1)$. For a Néel antiferromagnet, the inelastic weight is $s + δ$, where $δ$ is the zero-point reduction of the ordered moment. The sum rule then holds only to leading order in $1/s$.
  • Classical spin dynamics conserves the classical magnitude $|𝐒|^2 = s^2$, which is the total obtained with kT = nothing. With finite kT, the classical-to-quantum rescaling of intensities modifies the total. For static correlations, set_spin_rescaling_for_static_sum_rule! enforces the quantum value $s(s+1)$.
